## Abstract

This project documents my intentional strategies to make computer science and artificial intelligence accessible, supportive, and inclusive for all learners. Technical computing disciplines are historically plagued by high attrition and equity gaps, often intimidating non-traditional, female, and first-generation college students who lack prior exposure to code. To dismantle this barrier, I implemented "no-code" visual on-ramps in introductory courses like COSC 1010 and AIML 1010. By grounding initial units in logic puzzles, system flowcharts, and real-world ethical problem-solving, students discover that computational thinking is a human skill rather than an innate talent. Complementing this curricular on-ramp is an intentional, high-touch communication protocol: I maintain a strict 12-to-24-hour email response commitment and post weekly unedited video check-ins that humanize the online space. The quantitative results from Anthology Student Course Questionnaires (SCQs) validate this approach, showing consistent average scores between 3.86 and 4.00 out of 4.00 on the "welcoming and inclusive" metric across all terms. This project showcases how cultivating psychological safety, normalizing mistakes, and providing transparent support allows diverse students to thrive in rigorous STEM pathways.

## Project Reflection

### 1. Examination of Artifact Selection
These three artifacts provide a complete view of how an inclusive learning environment is systematically built. Artifact 1 provides verified student voice and institutional evaluation data confirming that students feel welcomed and supported. Artifact 2 illustrates the pedagogical mechanisms used to lower entry barriers for beginners. Artifact 3 demonstrates the day-to-day relational communication that maintains respect, reduces imposter syndrome, and builds student trust.

### 2. Growth Demonstrated in Relation to Student Environment
When I began teaching, I assumed that being approachable during scheduled office hours was sufficient to create an inclusive environment. However, I observed that the students who needed help the most were often too intimidated to attend office hours or ask questions in public forums. I realized that inclusivity requires proactive, deliberate outreach. I instituted early-semester check-in emails, simplified technical jargon, and restructured my introductory units to guarantee early wins for students from non-technical backgrounds.

### 3. Key Lessons Learned
Creating a welcoming environment in STEM is fundamentally about lowering the emotional cost of making mistakes. When students believe that errors reflect a lack of intelligence, they disengage and withdraw. By openly sharing my own debugging struggles and celebrating creative failed attempts in class, students learn that troubleshooting is the core work of a computer scientist. Following CET coaching from Sheridan Hanson (April 2024 observation), I also learned the power of chunking instructional videos to under 10 minutes, making content digestible for working adult students and parents juggling tight schedules.

### 4. Planned Changes and Future Steps
Looking forward, I intend to conduct comprehensive accessibility audits on all digital course assets in partnership with LCCC Disability Support Services. Additionally, I plan to integrate case studies highlighting contributions from underrepresented pioneers in computing and AI ethics into our foundational courses.

### 5. Broader Institutional and Community Impact
Cultivating an inclusive classroom directly drives retention in LCCC's STEM pathways. Non-traditional and adult students who might otherwise have abandoned technology degrees persist and transition into our advanced AI and Computer Science sequences, building a more diverse local tech workforce for Wyoming.
