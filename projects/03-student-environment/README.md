## Abstract

This project documents my intentional strategies to make computer science and artificial intelligence accessible, supportive, and inclusive for all learners. Technical computing disciplines are historically plagued by high attrition and equity gaps, often intimidating non-traditional, female, and first-generation college students who lack prior exposure to code. To dismantle this barrier, I implemented "no-code" visual on-ramps in introductory courses like COSC 1010 and AIML 1010. By grounding initial units in logic puzzles, system flowcharts, and real-world ethical problem-solving, students discover that computational thinking is a human skill rather than an innate talent. Complementing this curricular on-ramp is an intentional, high-touch communication protocol: I maintain a strict 12-to-24-hour email response commitment and post weekly unedited video check-ins that humanize the online space. The quantitative results from Anthology Student Course Questionnaires (SCQs) validate this approach, showing consistent average scores between 3.86 and 4.00 out of 4.00 on the "welcoming and inclusive" metric across all terms. This project showcases how cultivating psychological safety, normalizing mistakes, and providing transparent support allows diverse students to thrive in rigorous STEM pathways.

## Project Reflection

### 1. Examination of Artifact Selection
These artifacts demonstrate how an inclusive classroom environment is built intentionally from multiple angles: verified institutional evaluation metrics (Artifact 1), pedagogical design that removes barriers for beginners (Artifact 2), and continuous empathetic communication that reduces anxiety (Artifact 3). Together, they prove that a supportive classroom culture is an active, engineered instructional strategy.

### 2. Growth Demonstrated in Relation to Student Environment
When I first entered higher education instruction, I assumed that maintaining scheduled office hours and being polite in class was sufficient to create an inclusive environment. However, student observations and survey feedback revealed that the students most at risk of dropping out—often first-generation learners or students with no prior STEM background—felt intimidated by the traditional academic posture of office hours. I grew to realize that inclusivity requires proactive outreach. I transformed my communications into conversational, unedited check-ins, actively reached out to students after their first missed assignment, and redesigned introductory modules to ensure early success.

### 3. Key Lessons Learned
In computer science, student persistence is directly linked to psychological safety. When students feel that a syntax error implies they are not "cut out" for tech, they disengage. By publicly sharing my own coding mistakes during live lectures and celebrating novel debugging attempts, I help students reframe failure as the core diagnostic engine of computer programming.

### 4. Planned Changes and Future Steps
I plan to conduct formal accessibility audits across all digital course materials in partnership with LCCC Disability Support Services, ensuring universal design for screen readers and closed captioning. Additionally, I intend to introduce modules highlighting diverse pioneers in artificial intelligence and computing history.

### 5. Broader Institutional and Community Impact
Creating an accessible, supportive learning climate directly expands LCCC's STEM enrollment pipeline. Adult learners, women, and non-traditional students who initially doubt their technical capability gain the confidence to complete introductory courses and transition into advanced computer science and artificial intelligence credentials.
