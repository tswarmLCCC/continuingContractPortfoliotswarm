## Abstract

This project documents my faculty role in researching, designing, and implementing the first Associate of Applied Science (AAS) in Artificial Intelligence in the state of Wyoming, officially initiated in Fall 2025. Recognizing a rapid shift in workforce demands, I completed market research and collaborated with our regional Professional Advisory Committee (PAC) to design a state-aligned technical pathway. I developed curriculum specifications and syllabi for seven entirely new artificial intelligence (AIML) courses, alongside Data Engineering (COSC 2060) and the modernization of our computing pathways. Throughout this curriculum build, I integrated Bloom’s Taxonomy to scaffold student learning from foundational "no-code" logic and ethics to applied machine learning, computer vision, and neural models. The curriculum was vetted through collaboration with internal stakeholders, the PAC, and external partners, and through institutional and state approval processes. Following two presentations and defenses to the LCCC Board of Trustees, the Vice President of Academic Affairs (VPAA) conducted the formal curriculum defense. Funded in direct response to the Wyoming Innovation Partnership (WIP) Phase II grant, this program provides our community with accessible, industry-aligned credentials that bridge directly to high-demand technical careers.

## Project Reflection

### 1. Examination of Artifact Selection
These three artifacts were chosen because they trace the full lifecycle of academic program development: from external labor validation and state-level regulatory sanction (Artifact 1), down to granular classroom instructional scaffolding and continuous assessment refinement (Artifact 2), and responsive alignment through employer feedback (Artifact 3). Together, they prove that the AI program is not an isolated experiment, but a rigorous, sustainable, accredited pathway embedded within LCCC’s strategic vision.

### 2. Growth Demonstrated in Relation to Program Review
Developing a new degree accelerated my professional growth as a faculty member. Before joining LCCC, I had instructional expertise in both Computer Science and Data Science at the graduate level. Moving into an Associate-degree program required deliberate pedagogical adaptation: making advanced ideas accessible, sequencing learning in smaller steps, and connecting concepts to applied career skills for students at the two-year college level. I collaborated closely with the Director of Assessment, the Dean of Arts and Sciences, and the Professional Advisory Committee (PAC) as the curriculum was reviewed and refined. I presented the work to internal stakeholders, incorporated iterative feedback, and participated in external alignment discussions with the University of Wyoming (UW). I also presented and defended the work twice to the LCCC Board of Trustees. Following those presentations and defenses, the Vice President of Academic Affairs (VPAA) conducted the formal defense of the curriculum. This collaborative process helped me evolve from course-level grading to programmatic assessment design, refining complex and redundant outcomes into clear, measurable competencies that can be tracked across the degree.

### 3. Key Lessons Learned
The primary lesson from this build is that curriculum design is an iterative cycle of continuous refinement, not a one-and-done administrative task. Program competencies must be accessible, modular, and directly assessable. By collaborating with the Program Director and Director of Assessment to simplify and map our programmatic outcomes across course outcomes, we established clearer benchmarks for capstone evaluation. Anchoring the program in enduring foundations—algorithmic logic, ethical practice, and data pipeline fundamentals—allows it to adapt as technologies change. Staying current with fast-moving AI requires ongoing formal professional development through Intel and the National Applied AI Consortium (NAAIC), supplemented by self-directed study, especially as instruction evolves from large language models (LLMs) toward multi-level agentic AI.

### 4. Planned Changes and Future Steps
Moving forward, we will continue leveraging a Professional Advisory Committee, which will be combined with the Information Technology (IT) Pathway Professional Advisory Committee as the program transitions administratively and academically from the School of Arts and Sciences to the IT Pathway. Ongoing alignment with this unified advisory board will support a regular review of program outcomes and tools as AI, LLMs, and agentic paradigms advance. I will continue formal training through Intel and NAAIC, supplemented by self-directed study, to maintain the technical and instructional currency this degree requires. I am also collaborating with the University of Wyoming School of Computing on 2+2 articulation alignment so students pursuing a four-year bachelor's degree in computing or data science can transfer their credits effectively.

### 5. Broader Institutional and Community Impact
This program has helped position LCCC as a pioneer in community college AI education in Wyoming. It has contributed to state recognition and grant funding for our division, strengthened relationships with technology and academic partners, and, most importantly, created an accessible pathway for Wyoming residents to enter high-demand technical careers without leaving their home state.
