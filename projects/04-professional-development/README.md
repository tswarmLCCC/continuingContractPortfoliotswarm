## Abstract

This project showcases my continuous engagement with industry leaders, state working groups, and university partners to align LCCC’s technical education with emerging workforce demands. In rapidly accelerating disciplines like Artificial Intelligence and Computer Science, faculty development requires active learning and collaboration with industry and academic partners. I completed technical training through Intel and Dell's "AI for the Workforce" initiative and continue formal development through Intel and the National Applied AI Consortium (NAAIC), supplemented by self-directed study. This ongoing learning helps me keep pace with fast-emerging technologies, from large language models (LLMs) to multi-level agentic AI instruction, and informs how I adapt them for Associate-degree coursework. I also collaborated with faculty at the University of Wyoming's (UW) School of Computing on 2+2 articulation alignment and course crosswalks. This project features curricular integration matrices, articulation materials, and technical training records. Together, they demonstrate how continuing professional development strengthens partnerships, elevates instructional rigor, and equips LCCC students with competitive, high-demand workforce credentials.

## Project Reflection

### 1. Examination of Artifact Selection
These three artifacts illustrate the three essential dimensions of modern professional development in technical education: corporate industry alignment (Artifact 1), higher education transfer articulation (Artifact 2), and personal mastery of emerging technologies paired with community outreach (Artifact 3). Together, they prove that my development activities directly benefit students, academic pathways, and institutional standing.

### 2. Growth Demonstrated in Relation to Professional Development
Prior to the AI curriculum build, my professional learning was primarily centered on classroom pedagogical strategies. Engaging with initiatives such as Intel's AI for the Workforce and NAAIC training, alongside self-directed study, has helped me translate emerging technical practices into scaffolded, accessible coursework suitable for Associate-degree students. This is an ongoing process as AI develops from LLM-based applications toward multi-level agentic instruction.

### 3. Key Lessons Learned
The primary takeaway from collaborating with industry and university partners is that community college curriculum must remain agile and responsive. Maintaining close ties with external partners, alongside continuous self-training and formal development through Intel and NAAIC, helps me identify which skills are changing and how emerging tools can be taught responsibly and accessibly. Keeping pace with LLMs and newer agentic AI approaches is an essential part of maintaining currency in this degree program.

### 4. Planned Changes and Future Steps
I plan to deepen our relationship with regional employers by inviting industry software engineers to serve as guest reviewers for our student Capstone projects. I will also continue formal training through Intel and NAAIC, supplemented by self-directed study in LLMs and multi-level agentic AI instruction, alongside further learning in cloud data engineering, so the curriculum remains current and appropriate for Associate-degree students.

### 5. Broader Institutional and Community Impact
My professional collaborations have positioned LCCC as an active contributor to Wyoming's statewide technology ecosystem. By building seamless transfer agreements with UW and aligning with global industry standards, we ensure that LCCC students receive an education that is respected locally, regionally, and nationally.
