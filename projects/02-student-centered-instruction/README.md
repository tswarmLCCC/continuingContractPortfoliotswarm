## Abstract

This project demonstrates my student-centered approach to maximizing engagement and mastery across LCCC's Computer Science and Artificial Intelligence courses. In programming courses like Python Programming (COSC 2409) and Computer Science I (COSC 1030), I replaced conventional weekly homework policing with cumulative "Mini Programming Portfolios." This model gives students agency over their learning, allowing them to choose their own development environments and demonstrate mastery through a student-choice evaluation: an asynchronous screen-share video explanation or a live 1-on-1 virtual code conference. To help students build that conceptual confidence before evaluation, I also embed optional "Learn with AI" Socratic practice drills alongside weekly units—such as the "Professor Codd" SQL drill in Database Applications (CMAP 1815). These exercises give students explicit permission to say "I don't know" to an AI tutor, practice testing edge cases, and follow a strict Human-On-The-Loop (HOTL) protocol where every query must be executed in PostgreSQL 16 and defended in their own words. Together, the Socratic practice drills and portfolio conferences shift the classroom from transactional syntax checking to collaborative mentorship, eliminating exam panic and building real-world communication and critical AI-literacy skills.

## Project Reflection

### 1. Examination of Artifact Selection
These four artifacts were curated to show a complete, classroom-tested learning loop: **how students practice** (Artifact 3), **what they build** (Artifact 1), **how they are evaluated** (Artifact 2), and **how they experience the model** (Artifact 4). Artifact 1 establishes clear, transparent expectations that give students ownership of their work. Artifact 2 proves that rigor and empathy can coexist through flexible assessment modalities. Artifact 3 demonstrates how I scaffold daily conceptual struggle and AI literacy in the classroom so students aren't left alone with a blank screen or tempted to copy-paste AI answers. Artifact 4 provides direct student evidence that this approach lowers anxiety without lowering standards.

### 2. Growth Demonstrated in Relation to Student-Centered Instruction
Early in my instructional career, I relied heavily on automated grading platforms (like ZyBooks labs) to check syntax correctness. While efficient, this approach fostered transactional learning: students focused on getting green checkmarks rather than understanding algorithmic logic. Transitioning to Mini Programming Portfolios—and later adding Socratic "Learn with AI" practice drills—required me to re-engineer my role from a passive evaluator to an active diagnostic coach. Instead of policing whether students used outside tools, I built structured classroom habits that teach them *how* to interrogate an AI tutor, test edge cases against live systems, and explain their reasoning out loud.

### 3. Key Lessons Learned
The most profound lesson is that students write significantly better code—and think much more deeply—when they know they must explain it out loud. Automated autograders often incentivize copy-pasting and surface-level trial-and-error. When students practice with a Socratic AI persona first and then prepare for a five-minute code conference, they slow down and take time to understand variable scopes, query execution order, and data structures. In the CMAP 1815 debriefs, students didn't just learn SQL; they also caught when an AI gave weak context and compared SQL execution to math and C++—proof that structured struggle builds genuine critical thinking.

### 4. Planned Changes and Future Steps
To scale this model sustainably as program enrollment expands, I am developing an automated booking and rubric workflow for 1-on-1 code conferences. I am also expanding the "Learn with AI" Socratic drill library across all units of CMAP 1815 and introductory Python courses, refining prompts based on student feedback about which AI models handle Socratic questioning best, and training advanced second-year student tutors to conduct peer-level formative code reviews.

### 5. Broader Institutional and Community Impact
This combination of Socratic AI practice and dialogue-first assessment has virtually eliminated plagiarism issues within my sections, as students cannot pass without defending their work. More importantly, it equips LCCC students with the soft skills employers ask for most: the willingness to admit when they are stuck, the habit of verifying AI-generated output against real environments, and the ability to clearly explain technical decisions to another person.
