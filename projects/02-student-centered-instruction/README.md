## Abstract

This project demonstrates my student-centered approach to maximizing engagement and mastery across LCCC's Computer Science and Artificial Intelligence courses. In courses like Python Programming (COSC 2409) and Computer Science I (COSC 1030), I replaced conventional weekly homework policing with cumulative "Mini Programming Portfolios." This model gives students agency over their learning, allowing them to choose their own development environments and demonstrate mastery through a student-choice evaluation: an asynchronous screen-share video explanation or a live 1-on-1 virtual code conference. Student course evaluations consistently confirm that this dialogue-first model is far more personalized and less stressful than automated syntax grading. The portfolios transform evaluation into collaborative mentorship, eliminating the exam panic that frequently causes introductory programming students to withdraw. Furthermore, student-centered instruction requires active institutional advocacy. When a Spring 2026 administrative scheduling decision canceled a required database prerequisite, I designed a modern PostgreSQL curriculum over the summer and launched it as an independent study in Fall 2026 to keep three graduating students on track without delaying their degrees. This project exhibits my dedication to meeting learners where they are and removing barriers to their academic progression.

## Project Reflection

### 1. Examination of Artifact Selection
These artifacts demonstrate that student-centered instruction encompasses both classroom assessment design (Artifacts 1 & 2) and institutional student advocacy (Artifact 3). Artifact 1 shows how students are empowered to take ownership of their learning. Artifact 2 provides evidence of flexible assessment that accommodates diverse schedules and anxiety levels. Artifact 3 demonstrates responsiveness to student degree progression when institutional roadblocks occur.

### 2. Growth Demonstrated in Relation to Student-Centered Instruction
Early in my teaching career, I relied on automated code-grading platforms (such as ZyBooks labs). Although these tools provided rapid feedback, they created a transactional mindset: students coded to satisfy automated test suites without truly understanding algorithmic logic. Transitioning to Mini Programming Portfolios forced me to step into the role of a cognitive mentor. I shifted my focus from catching errors to asking diagnostic questions that guide students toward self-correction.

### 3. Key Lessons Learned
Students write far better code when they know they must verbally articulate their design decisions. Preparing for a five-minute code conference encourages learners to deeply understand loops, conditionals, and data structures rather than copy-pasting solutions. Moreover, offering the choice between asynchronous video and live conferencing accommodates students with high performance anxiety, neurodivergent needs, or conflicting work schedules.

### 4. Planned Changes and Future Steps
To scale this dialogue-based model sustainably as enrollment increases, I am implementing an automated booking and rubric management workflow. I also plan to train advanced second-year students to conduct formative peer code reviews, giving beginners early practice with collaborative software engineering workflows.

### 5. Broader Institutional and Community Impact
This model has virtually eliminated plagiarism in my sections while maintaining pass rates above 95% in courses like Python Programming. By treating evaluation as a collaborative dialogue, students leave LCCC confident in their technical communication skills—one of the qualities most prized by tech employers.
