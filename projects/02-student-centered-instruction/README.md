## Abstract

This project demonstrates my student-centered approach to maximizing engagement and mastery across LCCC's Computer Science and Artificial Intelligence courses. In courses like Python Programming (COSC 2409) and Computer Science I (COSC 1030), I replaced conventional weekly homework policing with cumulative "Mini Programming Portfolios." This model gives students agency over their learning, allowing them to choose their own development environments and demonstrate mastery through a student-choice evaluation: an asynchronous screen-share video explanation or a live 1-on-1 virtual code conference. Student course evaluations consistently confirm that this dialogue-first model is far more personalized and less stressful than automated syntax grading. The portfolios transform evaluation into collaborative mentorship, eliminating the exam panic that frequently causes introductory programming students to withdraw. Furthermore, student-centered instruction requires active institutional advocacy. When a Spring 2026 administrative scheduling decision canceled a required database prerequisite, I designed a modern PostgreSQL curriculum over the summer and launched it as an independent study in Fall 2026 to keep three graduating students on track without delaying their degrees. This project exhibits my dedication to meeting learners where they are and removing barriers to their academic progression.

## Project Reflection

### 1. Examination of Artifact Selection
These artifacts were curated to demonstrate that student-centered instruction is both an assessment philosophy (Artifacts 1 & 2) and an active ethic of advocacy (Artifact 3). Artifact 1 shows how clear, transparent expectations empower students to take ownership of their work. Artifact 2 proves that rigor and empathy can coexist through flexible assessment modalities. Artifact 3 documents the direct, tangible impact of student advocacy on retention and degree completion.

### 2. Growth Demonstrated in Relation to Student-Centered Instruction
Early in my instructional career, I relied heavily on automated grading platforms (like ZyBooks labs) to check syntax correctness. While efficient, this approach fostered transactional learning: students focused on getting green checkmarks rather than understanding algorithmic logic. Transitioning to Mini Programming Portfolios required me to re-engineer my entire assessment model. I shifted from a passive evaluator to an active diagnostic coach, listening to students articulate their thought processes and intervening directly when conceptual hurdles arose.

### 3. Key Lessons Learned
The most profound lesson is that students write significantly better code when they know they must explain it out loud. Automated autograders often incentivize copy-pasting and surface-level trial-and-error. When students prepare for a five-minute code conference, they take time to understand their own variable scopes, loops, and data structures. Furthermore, offering the choice between asynchronous video and live conferencing accommodates diverse schedules, neurodivergent learning preferences, and anxiety levels.

### 4. Planned Changes and Future Steps
To scale this model sustainably as program enrollment expands, I am developing an automated booking and rubric workflow. I also plan to train advanced, second-year student tutors to conduct peer-level formative code reviews, introducing students to the collaborative code-review practices standard in the software industry.

### 5. Broader Institutional and Community Impact
This dialogue-first assessment model has virtually eliminated plagiarism issues within my sections, as students cannot pass without defending their code. More importantly, it has boosted student persistence: students know their instructor is an accessible partner in their learning journey rather than a gatekeeper.
